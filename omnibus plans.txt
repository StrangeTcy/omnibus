Right. Then the idea graph isn't merely a memory component of Omnibus. It's the evolving model of your intellectual world that the system uses to decide what might be valuable to you next.

That changes the design considerably. A library tracker is one data source. A task DAG is a way to organize execution. Neither is the central abstraction.

The central abstraction is a personal idea hypergraph, continuously updated from what you encounter, what you think about it, and what you want to explore.

## 1. What the graph represents

I'd distinguish five kinds of objects:

Ideas

Concepts, claims, questions, theories, arguments, examples, and connections between them.

Resources

Books, chapters, videos, interviews, podcasts, articles, music, people, and particular passages or timestamps.

Encounters

What you've read, watched, listened to, asked about, revisited, enjoyed, or abandoned.

Your evolving interests

Topics you're curious about, concepts you already know, preferred presentation styles, current questions, and explicit goals.

Evidence and provenance

Where an idea came from, which passage supports a connection, how confident the system is, and whether you confirmed an inference.

These are connected through typed hyperedges. A hyperedge can relate several ideas to several resources, rather than forcing everything into isolated pairs.

For example, the graph might represent that a particular chapter of Milewski introduces a set of concepts, that a YouTube lecture covers substantially the same set, that one of the lecture's worked examples adds something new, and that you have already read the corresponding chapter.

That is a much more informative structure than either a collection of bookmarks or a flat list of reading progress.

## 2. The system should optimize marginal value, not consumption

Your examples reveal three distinct operations the graph needs to support.

### Avoid spending your attention twice

You're halfway through Milewski's book. The system knows that several lectures cover the same material.

It should be able to say:

> You've covered roughly half the book. These lectures substantially overlap with what you've read, so you can skip them without losing much. This other lecture has a useful worked example that the book treats briefly.

That requires more than matching titles. The system needs a map of which ideas each resource covers and where the coverage overlaps.

### Find useful connections at the right moment

You're reading Gödel, Escher, Bach.

> You're at the Crab Canon. This is a good time to listen to the corresponding musical piece. Here's a recording.

The recommendation is valuable not just because the music is related to the book, but because the relationship becomes useful at your current point in the book.

A static recommendation list cannot capture this as well as a graph combined with your current activity.

### Keep an interest alive without making it an obligation

You watch a Lex Fridman interview and indicate that you enjoyed it. The graph records the guest, topics, ideas, format, and your reaction.

Later, it finds a newer interview with the same person by another host.

> You liked the Fridman interview with this person. Patel has a newer conversation with them. It overlaps on these topics but covers these other subjects that weren't discussed in the first one.

The system doesn't need to know that every new interview is worth watching. It needs enough information to explain why this particular one might be.

## 3. How recommendations should work

I'd make every recommendation a comparison between what you already know or have encountered and what a candidate resource would add.

A candidate can have several kinds of value:

| Value         | Question                                                                            |
| ------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Novelty       | Does it introduce ideas or evidence you haven't encountered?                        |
| Connection    | Does it link two interests or bodies of knowledge that you currently keep separate? |
| Reinforcement | Does another medium help clarify something difficult?                               |
| Enjoyment     | Is it likely to appeal to your tastes?                                              |
| Timing        | Is it especially relevant to what you're reading, watching, or working on now?      |
| Cost          | How much time and effort would it require?                                          |
| Redundancy    | How much would it repeat material you already know?                                 |

These are not a universal scoring formula for human development. Their weights should depend on the situation and what you fuckingly respond to.

Sometimes you'll want something entirely new. Sometimes you'll want to deepen an existing interest. Sometimes you'll want the most enjoyable next book, regardless of whether it contributes much to any intellectual project.

The system should not silently decide that learning more is always better than having fun.

And since you said “a bit, not overly,” it should have a recommendation budget: offer a particularly relevant suggestion when there's a good opportunity, avoid repeating suggestions you've ignored, and let you dismiss, defer, or accept them without turning every leisure activity into homework.

## 4. What this means for Omnibus's architecture

I would now organize the project around four cooperating subsystems.

Idea hypergraph

The canonical semantic model: what exists, how ideas relate, what sources say, and what you have encountered.

Ingestion

Read files, import metadata and transcripts, process materials, and record your activity.

Inference

Identify overlaps, new ideas, prerequisites, connections, and candidate recommendations.

Personal interface

Answer questions about your intellectual world and offer useful next steps with reasons and links.

The task DAG sits alongside this as an execution mechanism. When a project requires work, the runtime can produce a plan. The DAG is not the canonical representation of your life, interests, or knowledge.

The Epistemic Compiler remains a specialized research workflow that can contribute its outputs to the graph. The Machine remains a distinct project with its own architecture. Omnibus is the personal system connecting these capabilities.

One implementation detail matters: the graph should preserve the distinction between what a source says, what the system infers, and what you believe. If a model claims that two lectures are equivalent, that's a hypothesis backed by evidence, not an established fact merely because the graph contains an edge.

## 5. What I'd build first

I would still start with books and media, but for a different reason than I suggested earlier. They're a good first domain in which to prove that the graph has practical value.

The first vertical slice should let you:

1. Import a small selection of books and videos, including their accessible text, metadata, or transcripts.

2. Extract ideas and map which portions of each resource cover them, retaining provenance.

3. Record your progress and reactions.

4. Compare two resources for overlap and additional material.

5. Produce a useful, context-sensitive next recommendation, with a link and an explanation.

6. Display the relevant part of the idea graph so you can inspect why it recommended something.

It should work without a paid API. Deterministic local processing handles metadata, progress, graph queries, and basic overlap calculations; any model-dependent extraction or reasoning should use a replaceable backend that respects your available free access.

The first test isn't whether the graph visualization looks impressive. It's whether, after you provide a small amount of real material and progress data, Omnibus can make one recommendation that saves you time or connects two things in a way you appreciate.

Then expand to other domains. The same graph can connect an interview to a book, a book to a piece of music, a mathematical idea to a programming problem, or a building you photographed to an architectural movement.

That's the design target: not a tracker that reports what you've done, but a system that uses an evolving model of your intellectual world to help you decide what is worth doing next.

# Omnibus: Idea Hypergraph and Personal Intellectual Assistant — Arena Build Spec

**Target:** extend the existing Omnibus repository in place.  
**Milestone:** V0.2 — an end-to-end, graph-informed personal knowledge capability.  
**Primary user:** one person, with multiple computers and a hard budget of zero monetary spend.  
**Operating principle:** use available deterministic tools first; use a model only where semantic judgment is required and only through an explicitly configured, genuinely available backend.

## 0. The product being built

Omnibus is intended to become a general-purpose personal assistant. Its central persistent representation should be an **inspectable idea hypergraph** that connects resources, ideas, people, topics, experiences, preferences, goals, and evidence. It should use this representation to help the user decide what is worth doing next, avoid redundant effort, and notice useful connections at the right moment.

This is not merely a reading tracker, bookmark manager, chat-history search, or task DAG. Those may be capabilities or internal mechanisms. The graph is the shared semantic model; the runtime is responsible for executing tasks; domain capabilities provide concrete functionality.

Illustrative desired interactions:

- The user reads the first half of a book by Milewski. Omnibus records the progress and can say that several supplied or discovered lectures overlap with material already covered, while identifying any lecture that adds something substantive.
- The user reads *Gödel, Escher, Bach* near the Crab Canon. Omnibus can recommend a suitable recording of the corresponding musical piece, with an explanation of the connection and a usable link.
- The user watches an interview with a guest and says they liked it. Later Omnibus can suggest a newer interview with the same person, explain how its topics overlap or differ, and link to it.
- The user can inspect the graph and provenance, correct a bad connection, reject a recommendation, or state that two resources were not actually redundant.

The system should optimize the user's **marginal value from their attention**, balancing novelty, useful reinforcement, cross-domain connections, interest, timing, effort, and redundancy. It must not turn leisure into compulsory self-improvement. Recommendations should be sparse, optional, explainable, and easy to dismiss.

No hidden mock fallback, browser-session scraping, undocumented endpoint exploit, or misleading “AI-powered” status. If no semantic backend is configured, clearly expose what is unavailable and keep deterministic/manual workflows functional.
5. **Respect provenance.** Distinguish observed metadata, source text, user assertions/reactions, deterministic calculations, model-generated hypotheses, and user-confirmed relationships. ## 4. Hypergraph model

Use a relational representation in the existing SQLite database unless the current implementation already has a suitable graph store. The representation must support **hyperedges with arbitrary cardinality**; do not label a plain pairwise graph “a hypergraph.” Keep it simple and queryable.

Minimum entities (adapt naming to repository conventions):

### `resource`
A book, book section, lecture, video, recording, interview, article, person, or other identifiable resource. Fields should include stable ID, resource type, title, creator(s), URL/path, publication date if known, language if known, metadata, created/updated timestamps, and source provenance. A resource may have parent/child structure (book → chapter; video → segment) without forcing every resource into a tree.

### `concept`
An idea, claim, question, method, entity, motif, technique, or topic. Keep the kind explicit. Do not collapse a source's assertion into the user's belief. Support aliases and merge/correction without losing history.

### `hyperedge` and `hyperedge_member`
A hyperedge has a type, status, provenance, confidence, optional valid-time range, and explanation. Its membership table identifies multiple participating nodes and the role each plays. Support types such as:

- `covers`: a resource/segment covers one or more concepts;
- `prerequisite_for`: concepts related by a prerequisite relation;
- `overlaps_with`: resources overlap in subject matter, supported by covered concepts or source passages;
- `adds_material`: one resource provides substantive material absent from another;
- `illustrates`: an example or recording illustrates a concept/motif;
- `created_by` / `features_person`: resource and people/creators;
- `related_to`: a weaker, explicitly typed relation when a stronger claim is unsupported;
- `user_likes` / `user_dislikes` / `user_wants_to_explore`: a user reaction or preference, with timestamp and provenance.

Avoid turning every association into `related_to`. Use meaningful relation types and record uncertainty.

### `content_unit`
A page, chapter, section, transcript segment, timestamp range, or other subresource with stable locator where available. Store extracted text and content hashes only when lawful and appropriate; do not duplicate full copyrighted books unnecessarily. Keep short evidence excerpts or offsets sufficient to show why a relationship was inferred.

### `activity_event`
Append-only events such as reading progress, watching/listening progress, completion, revisit, rating, dismissal, user correction, or goal change. Store event time, source, start/end location when known, normalized fraction where valid, and relevant metadata. Corrections append a new event or correction record; do not silently rewrite away the history.

### `user_preference`
Explicit preferences and inferred preferences must remain distinct. Store scope, statement/value, confidence, provenance, and whether the user confirmed it. A single ignored recommendation is weak evidence, not proof of disinterest.

### `recommendation`
Candidate resource/action, reason/features, supporting graph paths or evidence, estimated overlap/novelty, effort, confidence, status (proposed/seen/accepted/dismissed/completed/expired), and timestamps. Never repeatedly surface a dismissed item unless circumstances materially change or the user asks.

If the current schema already has an appropriate entity (for example, `artifact` or `event`), extend/reuse it where that preserves clear semantics. Add reversible SQLite migrations and tests. Avoid redundant parallel stores for the same information.

## 5. Provenance and epistemic status

Every imported field and graph relationship must be distinguishable as one of:

- observed directly from a local file or supported metadata source;
- extracted from accessible text/transcript, with a source locator/hash;
- explicitly entered or corrected by the user;
- deterministically calculated (such as progress fraction);
- inferred by a model, pending user validation;
- inferred heuristically, with its limits stated.

Model-derived semantic data must use a structured schema and include evidence references to available input. Invalid model output must be rejected or quarantined, not silently coerced into trusted graph state. The UI should let the user inspect, accept, edit, reject, merge, or disconnect proposed concepts/relations. Never assert two resources are “the same” merely because their titles or tags match.

## 6. Local Books ingestion and progress

Implement an ingestion interface and working scanner for a configured Books directory.

- Support files whose parsers are available in the current dependency set, prioritizing EPUB, PDF, HTML, Markdown, and plain text. Detect formats and expose unsupported formats explicitly rather than pretending to have read them.
- EPUB: extract embedded metadata and chapter/section structure using appropriate archive/XML parsing; preserve file identity and a stable content hash.
- PDF: extract metadata/page count and text where a supported parser is available. Page positions are valid units for a fixed PDF.

## 7. Resource import and semantic enrichment

A resource can be added by file, URL, or manual entry. In this milestone:

- Local text can be parsed as above.
- A URL resource stores the URL and supplied metadata. Retrieve public title/metadata only through available, ordinary documented mechanisms where feasible; handle failures gracefully.
- Transcripts or text can be imported from files or pasted by the user. If a YouTube transcript or music recording cannot be acquired through an existing accessible and permitted route, state that and allow manual linking/import. Do not promise a working YouTube integration unless tested.
- Keep links as URLs and never fabricate destination URLs. A link found by external search needs to be preserved as a sourced candidate.

Define a replaceable `SemanticEnricher` interface accepting accessible text/metadata plus relevant existing graph context, returning structured proposals for concepts, covered units, relationships, and evidence locators. It must be callable through a currently available configured backend; no particular commercial API or the Codex SDK is required. No paid provider can be required for the core application.

If no configured semantic backend is available:

- the scanner/progress/graph/query/dashboard must still work;
- the user can add/edit concepts and connect resources manually;
- candidate recommendations may use already-established graph relationships;
- the interface must clearly mark model-dependent enrichment unavailable;
- tests use a deterministic fake enricher only inside tests/fixtures, clearly separated from production configuration.

Do not use keyword overlap alone to claim semantic equivalence. Lexical overlap can propose a low-confidence candidate, explicitly labeled as such.

## 8. Recommendation engine: marginal value, not a reading quota

Implement a transparent first recommendation policy using known graph relationships and recorded user state. It need not be a sophisticated recommender, but it must be real, queryable, and explainable.

Candidate dimensions:

- relevance to a current resource, concept, project, or explicit interest;
- novelty relative to resources and concepts already encountered;
- overlap with completed/consumed material;
- substantive unique material potentially added;
- timing (for example, a specific book chapter currently in progress);
- estimated attention cost;
- explicit likes/dislikes and feedback on previous recommendations.

Prefer a small set of high-value candidates to an endless feed. Provide defaults that keep nudges sparse and let the user adjust or disable recommendations. Ignored suggestions reduce priority weakly; explicit dismissal should suppress repeats. Respect enjoyment as a legitimate objective; do not claim to optimize a person's intellectual evolution objectively.

Each surfaced recommendation must answer “why this, why now?” and expose evidence. Examples:

- “This lecture covers concepts also present in the chapters you marked read; estimated overlap is high. It may be skippable unless you want a second explanation.”
- “This section is associated with the Crab Canon. This linked recording may be useful at your current point.”
- “This interview features the same guest and is newer than the one you liked. These topics overlap; these others appear additional.”

These are target interaction examples, not claims that the required resources are already indexed. If the graph does not contain enough evidence, ask for input or offer a clearly labeled candidate—not a confident recommendation.

Store recommendation explanations in structured form, including evidence paths, so they remain inspectable after the model response is gone.

## 13. Definition of done and final report

The milestone is done only when all are true:

- Omnibus demonstrably stores and queries a real n-ary idea hypergraph.
- A user can scan a local Books folder, inspect discovered resources, and record progress.
- Progress and a chart survive application restart.
- The UI displays an inspectable graph neighborhood with source/provenance data.
- A contextual recommendation is generated from known relationships and user state, with an explanation, not just a generic LLM suggestion.
- The user can correct relationships and dismiss recommendations, and those actions affect future outputs.
- Core functionality requires no model call and no paid API.
- No original book files are changed.
- Existing tests plus new tests pass, or failures are clearly reported without false success claims.

Final report must name changed files, migrations, test commands and raw pass/fail counts, actual manual smoke-test steps/results, supported/unsupported file types, whether any real semantic backend was configured and tested, and unresolved defects. Never claim successful semantic discovery based solely on synthetic fixtures or a fake worker.
